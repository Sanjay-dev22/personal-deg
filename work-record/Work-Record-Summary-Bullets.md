## Week 10 (14–15 Oct) — The aggregator actually built, rebuilt properly, and stress-tested until it broke — twice

- Built a first working aggregator: a new dashboard that discovers small sellers' offers, pools several into one larger block, publishes it, and later splits the real settlement back out to each original small seller in proportion to their own price — verified with a real live multi-seller trade reconciling to the rupee both ways
- The user then actually used it themselves and found two real problems no amount of code-reading had caught: generic, unhelpful error messages hiding the real rejection reason underneath, and a genuine security gap — the dashboard was letting a block be pooled and resold out of offers that didn't actually belong to this seller at all, from the wider shared network. Both fixed and verified live, with clear role-explanation banners added to all three dashboards so it's always obvious who is supposed to be doing what
- Recognised this first version still had a structural problem the user put directly: the aggregator's own pooled block was showing up inside the individual seller's own dashboard, which doesn't match how a real aggregator should work. Rebuilt it properly ("Aggregation v2"): the aggregator now has its own fully independent network identity and its own catalog, completely separate from any individual seller's — reusing an already-registered identity rather than requiring new government registration, confirmed safe to do
- Added demand-first matching: a fourth dashboard representing the grid/utility buyer side, which posts what it actually needs (quantity and a maximum price) before anything is pooled, and the aggregator matches its available small sellers against that real stated need — mirroring how real-world flexibility markets actually work, rather than pooling first and hoping a buyer turns up
- The user then pointed out a further logical gap: the party stating the need (the grid/utility dashboard) wasn't the party actually buying — the ordinary bulk buyer dashboard was still doing that instead. Fixed by giving the grid/utility dashboard its own independent network identity too, so it now discovers and buys the matching block itself, closing the loop
- Before even reporting this as finished, deliberately tried to break the new design with a battery of live test scenarios and found four more real, distinct bugs: a demand request could get permanently stuck unfulfilled if the pool published to serve it was smaller than what was actually needed; a small seller's offer could in principle be pooled into two different blocks at once; a block's final price (after the aggregator's own margin) was never actually checked against the buyer's own stated price ceiling, so a buyer could silently end up paying more than they'd said they would; and a safety rule meant to stop a single relabelled offer being called "pooled" was blocking a legitimate later top-up of an already-genuine multi-seller pool. All four fixed and re-verified live, including the exact scenario that first surfaced the problem (a partially-filled pool, topped up hours later by a newly-arrived seller, correctly completing the original request)
- Separately found and fixed four smaller but real display bugs, all sharing one root cause (a dashboard quietly refreshing itself every few seconds and wiping out something the user had just clicked open or started typing before they could act on it) — including, in one case, a genuine double-submission risk where clicking "buy" twice in quick succession could have sent two real purchases for the same need
- Finished by researching, with real current sources rather than assumption, exactly how far this mechanism is from a real national rollout — confirming this isn't hypothetical (a real government-run pilot of this exact pattern, with a real farmer selling to a real shop buyer, was already demonstrated live and shown to the Prime Minister in February 2026) — and laid out, in priority order, what would actually have to exist first: real metering (not a typed-in number), real identity verification for each participant, a real payment rail, a real dispute process, and a settled regulatory path specifically for the aggregator's own role, which doesn't fully exist yet even in the real pilot programs
